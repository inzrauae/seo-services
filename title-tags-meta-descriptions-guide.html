Title Tags and Meta Descriptions That Actually Get Clicks

Ranking is only half the job. The other half happens in the two lines of blue text that decide whether someone actually clicks through to you or the result above you.

By Buddika S. Weerasekara, Founder & CEO, Applantics (Pvt) Ltd · 6 September 2026

Why click-through rate is a ranking input too

Two pages can rank in the same position and earn wildly different amounts of traffic, purely because of how their title and description read in the results list. Google also uses engagement signals as part of how it continues to evaluate a page over time, so a title that consistently underperforms its position can hold a page back even after the ranking work is otherwise done.

What makes a title tag work

Put the primary keyword near the front, since that is what a scanning eye catches first and what Google bolds when it matches the query. Keep it specific rather than generic: "Wedding Photographer in Colombo" beats "Home | Photography Services" because it tells the searcher immediately that this result matches their exact intent. Aim for roughly 50 to 60 characters so the full title survives truncation in the result.

Writing meta descriptions that get clicked

A meta description is not a summary, it is a pitch. State the specific benefit or answer the page delivers, include a concrete detail, a number, a location, a timeframe, that a generic competing result will not have, and end with a reason to click rather than trailing off. Google rewrites the displayed snippet from on-page text a large share of the time when it judges that text a closer match to the query, so treat the description as your best attempt rather than a guarantee of what shows.

Common mistakes

  • Duplicate titles across multiple pages, which makes it harder for Google to know which page should rank and looks identical and unhelpful in a results list.
  • Keyword stuffing a title with the same term repeated, which reads as spam to a human and gains nothing over one clean mention.
  • Writing a title so long it gets truncated mid-word, cutting off the exact detail meant to earn the click.
  • Leaving the meta description blank and letting Google auto-generate one, which is rarely as compelling as a deliberately written pitch.

Testing and iterating

Search Console's performance report shows impressions and clicks per page, which makes click-through rate directly measurable. A page with strong impressions but a low click-through rate relative to its position is the clearest signal that the title and description, not the ranking, are the problem worth fixing next. This is standard on-page SEO maintenance, not a one-time task.

The honest summary

A title and description are the only part of your ranking that a searcher actually reads before deciding whether to visit. Writing them as a genuine pitch to a human, backed by real keyword research into what that human actually typed, consistently outperforms writing them as a keyword checklist.

FAQ

Common Questions

How long should a title tag be?

Around 50 to 60 characters is the safe range before Google starts truncating it in the result, though the real limit is pixel width rather than character count, so a title full of wide capital letters can truncate slightly sooner than a mixed-case one of the same length.

Does Google always use the meta description I write?

No. Google frequently rewrites the displayed snippet using text pulled from the page if it judges that text a better match for the searcher's specific query. Writing a strong meta description still matters, since it is what shows for the majority of queries, but expect it to be overridden some of the time.

Should every page on a site have a unique title tag?

Yes. Duplicate title tags across multiple pages make it harder for Google to understand which page should rank for which query, and they look identical and unhelpful in a list of search results, which directly reduces the click-through rate of the pages involved.

Do title tags need the exact keyword or can they be more natural?

A natural phrasing that clearly includes the core keyword works better than an exact-match string forced in awkwardly. Google's systems handle close variants and synonyms well, and a title written for a human to click outperforms one written purely to match a keyword string.
