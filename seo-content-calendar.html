How Often Should You Publish? A Realistic SEO Content Calendar

Frequency is the question everyone asks and the wrong one to start with. Here is what actually correlates with ranking growth, and a calendar a small team can sustain.

By Buddika S. Weerasekara, Founder & CEO, Applantics (Pvt) Ltd · 6 September 2026

Quick Answer

There is no universal number of blog posts per month that guarantees rankings. Two to four genuinely useful posts a month, published consistently and built from real keyword research, outperform a burst of content followed by silence. Updating existing pages deserves equal priority to publishing new ones, since an already-ranking page is usually faster to improve than starting from zero.

Frequency is the wrong first question

"How many posts a week" assumes frequency is the lever that moves rankings. It is not, on its own. A site that publishes daily but never builds real depth on its subject competes worse than a site publishing twice a month that genuinely covers its topic. The right first question is what to cover, not how often to post.

What actually correlates with ranking growth

Topical coverage and consistency matter more than raw volume. Google's systems build a picture of which sites are genuine authorities on a subject by seeing sustained, connected coverage of it over time, not by counting publish dates. A burst of twenty posts in one month followed by six months of silence signals the opposite of authority: it looks abandoned, and traffic to those posts typically plateaus rather than compounding the way steady output does.

A realistic calendar for a small team

  • Two to four posts a month, published on a fixed schedule you can actually sustain for a year, not a burst you cannot repeat.
  • One update pass a month on existing content, refreshing statistics, adding sections competitors have since covered, and fixing anything that has gone out of date.
  • A quarterly review of which topics are actually driving traffic, so the calendar keeps adjusting to what is working rather than running on autopilot.

What to write about first

Start from keyword research, not from whatever topic feels interesting that week. List the specific questions your actual customers ask before buying, rank them by realistic difficulty against your site's current authority, and work down that list in order. This is the core of a proper content strategy: a calendar that follows demonstrated demand rather than internal opinion about what visitors probably want to read.

Seasonal and evergreen content: balancing the two

A calendar built entirely from evergreen topics misses the concentrated demand spikes around specific dates, festivals, tax deadlines, back-to-school periods, that a well-timed seasonal post can capture almost entirely on its own. A calendar built entirely from seasonal content, on the other hand, produces a site that goes quiet for most of the year and never accumulates the steady topical authority evergreen pages build.

A reasonable split for most small businesses is roughly three evergreen posts to every one seasonal post, with the seasonal piece published and internally linked at least a month before the date it targets, since Google needs time to crawl and index a page before the search volume for that date actually arrives.

Keeping older content alive

A post published two years ago and never touched since is quietly losing ground to competitors who have since covered the same topic more completely. Updating an existing page that already has some authority is often faster than writing something new from zero, since it starts from an existing ranking rather than none. Treat the update pass as equally important as new publishing, not as an afterthought squeezed in when time allows.

The honest summary

There is no universal number of posts per month that guarantees results. What reliably works is a pace you can sustain indefinitely, built around genuine topical coverage rather than filler, with as much attention paid to updating what already exists as to publishing what is new.

FAQ

Common Questions

How many blog posts per month does SEO actually require?

For most small businesses, two to four genuinely useful posts a month, published consistently, outperform a burst of ten posts followed by months of silence. Google rewards sustained topical coverage over time far more than raw volume in any single month.

Is it better to publish new content or update old content?

Both, on a rotation. New content is what expands your topical coverage into keywords you do not yet rank for. Updating older posts, refreshing statistics, adding sections competitors now cover, tends to produce faster ranking gains per hour of work, since the page already has some authority to build on.

Does posting less frequently hurt rankings?

A slower, sustainable pace does not hurt rankings on its own. What hurts is stopping entirely after an initial burst, or publishing so rarely that the site never builds enough topical coverage to be seen as a genuine authority on its subject.

How do I decide what to write about first?

Start from keyword research rather than from ideas that feel interesting to write. List the specific questions your actual customers ask before buying, rank them by realistic difficulty against your site's current authority, and work down that list in order rather than whatever topic comes to mind that week.

Should I prioritise seasonal content or evergreen content?

Evergreen content should make up the bulk of a calendar, since it accumulates steady traffic and authority year-round, while seasonal content captures a concentrated spike around a specific date. A rough split of three evergreen posts to one seasonal post works for most small businesses, with seasonal pieces published at least a month ahead of the date they target.
